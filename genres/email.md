# Email and letters

Messages to a supervisor, colleague, client, office or stranger. The reader decides in a few seconds whether to act, so the purpose goes first and the next step goes last.

## Ask first

- Who is the reader, and what is the relationship (stranger, superior, peer, friend)?
- What is the one thing the reader should do or know after reading?
- Is there a deadline or a date?
- Is this a first message or a reply? For a reply, ask to see the thread: what the reader already knows must not be explained back to them.
- Does the writer have an earlier email of their own that sounds right? Match it.

If the message has two unrelated purposes, propose two emails.

## Structure to build on

| Part | Rule |
|---|---|
| Subject line | A headline for the message: the topic and, when time matters, the date. "Chapter 2 draft: 20 minutes this week?" and never "Question" or "Hi". |
| Greeting | Matched to the relationship. When in doubt, the more formal form. |
| First sentence | The purpose: the request, the answer or the news. A stranger also needs one clause on who is writing. |
| Context | Only what this reader lacks in order to act. Quote the line being asked about and do not paraphrase the whole thread. |
| Details | One thought per short paragraph. Items the reader must bring, choose between or answer go in a list. |
| Close | The next step and its date: what the reader should send, decide or confirm, or what the writer will do next. |
| Sign-off | Matched to the greeting, then the name. A stranger also gets a role and an organisation. |

**Register pairs.** Formal British convention pairs "Dear Sir or Madam" with "Yours faithfully" and "Dear Ms Thompson" with "Yours sincerely". A first-name greeting takes "Kind regards" or "Best wishes". American usage puts a full stop after Mr., Mrs. and Ms. and closes with "Sincerely". Follow the reader's convention where it is known.

**Formal and informal are both correct.** "I am afraid I cannot manage next Monday" and "Sorry, can't make Monday" say the same thing to different readers. Pick one register from the relationship and hold it through the message. Contractions belong to the informal one.

**One polite modal per request.** "Could you send the form by Friday?" is polite and clear. Stacking softeners ("I was just wondering whether it might perhaps be possible") hides the request.

**Length.** As short as the purpose allows. A request that needs more than a screen of explanation is a meeting or an attached document, with an email that says so.

## The avoid list in email

| Move | Typical form in email | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Writing for the wrong reader | Two paragraphs of background the reader wrote or already agreed to, with the request in the last line | Lead with the request or the decision. Keep the one fact the reader lacks. |
| Staged opening | "I hope this email finds you well." "I wanted to reach out to touch base regarding ..." | Open with the purpose. A greeting line is enough courtesy. |
| Importance flag | "This is extremely important." "I cannot stress enough ..." | State the consequence and the date: "The portal closes on 28 November." |
| Praise without evidence | "your invaluable guidance", "your esteemed institution", "I truly appreciate everything" | Thank the reader for the specific thing: "Thank you for the comments on Section 2.3." |
| Formula ending | A closing paragraph that repeats the request; "Thank you for your time and consideration" after two other thanks | End on the next step. One thanks. |
| Chat residue | "Here is a draft you can use:", "[Your Name]", "Feel free to adjust the tone" | Remove. Check every bracket before sending. |
| Emphatic modifiers | "incredibly excited", "absolutely thrilled", "deeply grateful" | Say what will be done, or leave the feeling to one plain word. |

**Not defects here.** A salutation, a sign-off and the standard phrases of business correspondence are older than language models: "Thank you for your email of 4 June", "Please find attached", "I look forward to hearing from you". Keep them where they do a job. "Please do not hesitate to contact me" is correct when questions are likely and filler when it closes every message.

**Formatting.** Bold is acceptable on a single deadline or date. Whole sentences in bold or capitals read as shouting.

## Example

The scenario is invented for illustration. In real work every detail comes from the writer.

**Before**

> Subject: Meeting Request
>
> Dear Professor Lambert,
>
> I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to express my sincere gratitude for your invaluable guidance throughout my thesis journey. As you know, I have been working diligently on the second chapter, which plays a crucial role in the overall structure of the dissertation. I wanted to reach out to see if it might be possible to schedule a meeting at your earliest convenience to discuss the draft and gain your valuable insights.
>
> Thank you so much for your time and consideration. Please do not hesitate to let me know if you need any further information.
>
> Best regards,

**After**

> Subject: Chapter 2 draft: 20 minutes this week?
>
> Dear Professor Lambert,
>
> Could we meet for 20 minutes this week about the Chapter 2 draft I sent on 4 November? I have two questions:
>
> - Are the two hypotheses specific enough to test?
> - Does the section on sampling belong in Chapter 2 or in the method chapter?
>
> I am free on Wednesday after 14:00 and all day Friday, on campus or online.
>
> Kind regards,
> Sam Peeters

**What changed**

- The subject line now carries the topic, the size of the request and the time frame.
- The request moved from the fourth sentence to the first, with the date that identifies the draft.
- The thanks, the progress report and "plays a crucial role" told the supervisor nothing new and are gone.
- The close offers times, so the reader can answer in one line.
- The two questions came from the writer. A model cannot supply them.

## Checks

1. Read only the subject line and the first sentence. Is the purpose clear?
2. Is there exactly one request, and does it have a date where one applies?
3. Does any sentence tell the reader something they wrote or already know?
4. Do the greeting, the register and the sign-off belong to the same relationship?
5. Are all brackets and placeholders gone, and is the attachment attached?
