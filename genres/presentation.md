# Presentations

Slides, speaker notes and talk scripts. A listener cannot reread. Each slide has to make one point that can be taken in while someone is speaking, and the spoken text has to carry the links that a page of prose would show.

## Ask first

- Who is the audience, and what do they already know? The same term is useful detail to one audience and jargon to another.
- What is the presenter's goal: a decision, feedback on a method, a result to communicate, a defence?
- How long is the slot, and is there a slide limit or a template?
- What is the one message the audience should leave with?
- Which results, figures and data exist? Ask for the charts and their numbers.
- Is the request for slides, for a spoken script, or for both? They are written differently.

## Structure to build on

### The talk

1. **Open on a motivation the whole audience shares**, then move step by step to what was done and why. Too much background costs a few minutes. Too little loses the room for the whole talk.
2. **State the question** the work answers.
3. **Give each result its own slide**, and say what it means for the question.
4. **Tie each transition to the question.** "We had a result but did not know whether it held outside the pilot site, so we ..." orients the listener. "Next, we also did ..." does not.
5. **Close on the message** and, where a decision is wanted, on the request.

### The slide

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Title | A full sentence stating the slide's takeaway. A listener who reads only the titles, in order, should be able to follow the argument. |
| Body | The evidence for the title, shown: a chart, a diagram, a photo, a table of a few numbers. Use a short list only when the content is a list. |
| Text | In the best case the title is the only full sentence. Supporting text is a few words. Anything the presenter will say in full belongs in the notes. |
| Scope | One point per slide. A slide with two points becomes two slides. |
| Figure | Rebuilt for the room: only what proves the point, plain labels, the key series emphasised. A figure made for a paper usually carries too much. |

To find a title, finish the sentence "The point of this slide is that ...". What follows "that" is the title.

### The script

Spoken text may use first person and contractions, shorter sentences than written prose, and deliberate repetition of the key term. Before showing data, tell the audience what the axes are and what to look for.

**Describing figures.** State the number with its preposition: sales rose *by* 4%, *from* 20,000 *to* 30,000; inflation levelled off *at* 2.5%; a decrease *in* sales *of* 4%; the region accounts *for* 23% of revenue. "The line rises", "the X-axis shows time in months".

## The avoid list in slides and scripts

| Move | Typical form | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Topic in place of a claim | Titles such as "Overview", "Background", "Results", "Key Takeaways", "Challenges and Opportunities" | A sentence title that states the finding. |
| Praise without evidence | "A game-changing solution", "Unlocking the power of ..." | What it does, with the number. |
| Reflex triads | Three bullets on every slide, each a bold label and a phrase ("**Scalability:** ...") | The number of points the evidence supports, shown as evidence. |
| Importance flag | "Why this matters" as a slide with no figure on it | The consequence, as a number or a comparison. |
| Emphatic modifiers | "significant improvement", "dramatic growth", "enhanced satisfaction" | The size of the change on the slide. |
| Staged opening (script) | "Today, I'm excited to take you on a journey ...", "In today's fast-paced world ...", "Without further ado" | The motivation, in one concrete sentence. |
| Formula ending | A last slide that says only "Thank you" or "Questions?"; a "Conclusion" slide restating the agenda | A last slide that holds the message and, where relevant, the request. It stays on screen during questions. |
| Corrective frame | "It's not just about data. It's about people." | The claim itself. |
| Chat residue | "Slide 3:", "[Insert chart here]", "Speaker notes: feel free to adapt" | Remove, and supply or ask for the chart. |

**Not defects here.** Fragments in the slide body, large type and few words, a repeated layout, and repetition of the key message at the start and the end of the talk. In a talk, saying the main point twice helps a listener who cannot reread.

**Do not over-correct.** Do not move the full script onto the slides to "add substance", and do not strip a slide to a title with no evidence.

## Example

The study and its figures are invented for illustration, continuing the example in `genres/proposal.md`. In real work every detail comes from the writer.

**Before**

> **Results**
> - Significant improvement in attendance
> - Enhanced patient engagement
> - Promising implications for future scale-up

**After**

> **SMS reminders cut missed appointments from 14% to 9%**
> [Bar chart: two bars, "No reminder 14%" and "Reminder 9%"]
> 2,000 patients, one clinic, six months

Spoken with the slide:

> "Here's the main result. On the left, patients who got no reminder: fourteen percent missed their appointment. On the right, patients who got one text two days before: nine percent. That's five points lower, at one clinic, over six months."

**What changed**

- The title became the finding. A listener who sees nothing else on the slide still gets it.
- Three symmetric bullets became one chart, because there was one result.
- "Significant", "enhanced" and "promising" became the two numbers.
- The sample and setting sit under the chart in six words, so the limits of the result are visible.
- The script explains how to read the chart before giving the difference.

## Checks

1. Read the slide titles in order, alone. Do they tell the story?
2. Does each slide make exactly one point, and is its evidence on the slide?
3. Could the audience read every word on the slide in a few seconds?
4. Does every figure have labelled axes and a stated unit?
5. Is the last slide the message and the request?
6. Does the number of slides fit the slot?
