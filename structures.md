# Positive Structures

What competent academic prose does, stated as things to build. This file belongs to `genres/academic.md`; the other genres carry their structures in their own files. `patterns.md` lists what to remove, and most of its repairs are one of the structures below, applied.

The guidance is self-contained. Each section ends with a public source that teaches the convention at more length. The examples draw on the three published articles cited in `examples.md`, so every figure can be checked.

These are conventions that writing centres and style guides taught long before language models, for the reader's sake. Building a paragraph this way improves it and leaves fewer of the patterns in `patterns.md`. It is not a method for obtaining a detector score.

---

## 1. Claim, evidence, interpretation

A body paragraph answers three questions in order.

| Slot | Question it answers | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | What does this paragraph assert? | One sentence that a reader could check or dispute |
| Evidence | What supports it, and who says so? | A figure, finding, quotation or example, with its source |
| Interpretation | What does the evidence mean for the research question? | The writer's own reading, hedged as far as the evidence requires |

Evidence and interpretation can repeat inside one paragraph when a claim rests on several pieces of evidence. The paragraph ends on its newest information, when the last interpretation is made.

**Example**

> **[Claim]** Income tax became a mass tax in China between 1986 and 2008 and did not in India. **[Evidence]** The share of the Chinese population subject to the tax rose from less than 0.1% to about 20%, while the Indian share stayed at around 2-3% (Piketty & Qian, 2009). **[Interpretation]** The Indian figure probably reflects policy as much as income, because an exemption threshold that is revised upward removes new taxpayers as fast as growth adds them.

**How to build it**

1. Write the claim first, on its own. Test it: could a reader disagree, or look it up? "Tax policy is important for development" fails. The claim above passes.
2. Attach the evidence, with its source in the same sentence or the next. If the writer's material holds none, the claim is not ready. Ask for the evidence.
3. Write the interpretation by answering "what follows for my research question?" This sentence belongs to the writer. A model may question it and must not invent it.
4. Stop. Delete a final sentence that repeats the claim.

**By section**

| Section | The claim is usually | The evidence is usually | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | The problem, then the gap | Figures on scale; prior studies | Why the gap leads to this study's aim |
| Literature review | A position several sources share, or a disagreement between groups | The sources, grouped by position | What the grouping leaves unanswered |
| Methods | A design choice | The procedure, in enough detail to repeat | The reason for the choice, where it is not obvious |
| Results | A finding | The data, with a reference to the table or figure | Held back for the discussion |
| Discussion | An answer to the research question | The findings, set against prior work | Explanation, limits and implication |

**Source:** The Writing Center, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, "Paragraphs": https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/. It teaches the same order as five steps: a controlling idea in a topic sentence, elaboration, an example, an explanation of the example, and a completion or a transition. It notes that the example and explanation steps repeat as needed and that length does not define a paragraph.

## 2. Given-new flow

Readers take in a sentence most easily when it opens with something they already know and ends with what is new. They also give the end of the sentence the most weight, so the new and important item goes there.

| Device | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Repeat the key term | Open with the term the previous sentence ended on, unchanged | "... depends on the exemption threshold. The threshold ..." |
| "This" plus a noun | Name the previous sentence's content in one noun and open with it | "This gap", "These surveys", "Such critics" |
| Passive to reorder | Use the passive when it brings the known item to the front | "These surveys are designed to be representative of urban China" (Piketty & Qian, 2009, p. 54) |

**Example**

> Unlinked: India kept its taxpaying population near 2-3%. Furthermore, upward revisions of the exemption threshold took place.
>
> Linked: India kept its taxpaying population near 2-3%. It did so by revising the exemption threshold upward (Piketty & Qian, 2009).

The second version needs no transition, because its opening words point back and its closing words carry the new fact.

A longer chain is the literature-review paragraph in `examples.md` (pair 2). Its sentences open with "Defenders hold", "Critics reject the aim itself", "Rawls rejects it too" and "Rose (2020) starts from this gap". Each opener picks up something the previous sentence supplied, and the paragraph contains no additive connective.

**When a connective is needed.** Addition is the reader's default assumption and seldom needs a marker. Mark the relation when it would otherwise be misread: contrast and concession (however, yet, although, whereas), and a result whose inference is not obvious (therefore, consequently).

**At paragraph level.** The last sentence should still concern the subject the first one announced. If it has moved to another subject, the paragraph holds two points and should be split. This is a check for drift and does not call for restating the topic sentence.

**Source:** Duke Graduate School Scientific Writing Resource, Lesson 2, "Cohesion, Coherence, and Emphasis": https://sites.duke.edu/scientificwriting/lesson-2-cohesion-coherence-and-emphasis/. Its three principles are to put new information last, to use the passive when it moves old information to the front, and to make the first and last sentences of a paragraph match. The principle is usually traced to Gopen and Swan (1990), "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist*, 78, 550-558.

## 3. Hedging with a reason

A hedge tells the reader how far the evidence reaches. It is information, and it deserves the precision of a figure.

**What to hedge**

| Hedge | Leave unhedged |
|---|---|
| An explanation of a result | The procedure that was followed |
| A generalisation beyond the sample | The data that were observed |
| An implication, a recommendation, a prediction | What a cited source reported (choose an accurate reporting verb) |

**Match the wording to the evidence**

| Evidence behind the claim | Wording |
|---|---|
| Measured directly in this study | is, was, shows, found |
| A consistent pattern across studies, or a result that survives robustness checks | indicates, suggests, tends to, is likely to |
| One study, a small sample, or a plausible mechanism that was not tested | may, could, appears to, possibly |
| A conjecture offered for later work | might, it is possible that |

**Rules**

1. One hedge per claim. "May potentially suggest" stacks three on one verb.
2. State the reason for the doubt in the same sentence: because, given that, since. A hedge without a reason reads as reflex.
3. Tie each limitation to its consequence: what the study cannot conclude because of it.
4. For generalisations, prefer the number to the quantifier. "Most" becomes "14 of 19" where the count is known.
5. Use boosters (clearly, certainly) only after the evidence has been shown. They are uncommon in academic prose.

**Example**

> Reflex: While the projections are promising, various factors may potentially influence the outcomes.
>
> Reasoned: An upward revision of the exemption threshold would probably remove many new taxpayers at once, which is how India has kept its taxpaying population near 2-3%.

The second sentence carries one hedge ("would probably"), on one mechanism, with the precedent that justifies the doubt.

The same holds for stock limitation phrases. The Manchester Phrasebank lists "These data must be interpreted with caution because ...". The phrase is useful when the clause after "because" is filled in and empty when it is not.

**Sources:** University of Manchester, Academic Phrasebank, "Being cautious": https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/using-cautious-language/, which groups hedging phrases by task (explaining results, discussing implications, making recommendations, writing about the future, avoiding over-generalisation). George Mason University Writing Center, "Hedges: Softening Claims in Academic Writing": https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/guides/hedges-softening-claims-in-academic-writing, which lists the devices by word class and notes that boosters are less common in academic writing. The matching table and rules 1 to 3 are this skill's own; the two sources supply the devices.

## 4. Concrete subjects and verbs

Duke's writing resource gives three principles: put actions in verbs, put characters in subjects, and keep subjects near verbs. In academic prose the "character" is usually the thing studied: the threshold, the survey, revenues, the model, the author under discussion.

| Inflated frame | Concrete form |
|---|---|
| X plays a role in Y | What X does to Y: raises, lowers, delays, predicts |
| X has an impact on Y | The direction and the size: "X raised Y by ..." |
| X serves as a mechanism for Y | "X does Y", with the step named |
| There has been an increase in X | "X rose from ... to ..." |
| The implementation of X was carried out | "X was implemented", or "we implemented X" where the field accepts first person |
| X experienced remarkable growth | "X grew from ... to ..." |

**Example**

> China's income tax system has experienced remarkable growth. → Chinese income tax receipts grew from less than 0.1% of GDP in 1986 to 2.5% in 2008 (Piketty & Qian, 2009).

**Nominalisations.** A nominalisation is a noun that hides an action. Duke's pair: "We performed an analysis on the data" becomes "We analyzed the data." Keep the noun in two cases: when it names the previous sentence's action in order to open the next one ("This analysis ..."), which is a given-new link, and when it is the field's term (taxation, regression, attrition).

**Subject and verb together.** When more than about ten words separate the subject from its verb, move the qualifying material after the verb or into its own sentence.

**Reporting verbs.** The verb states what the source did. A source that measured something found, reported or estimated it. A source that took a position argues, holds or rejects it. A source that was tentative suggests or proposes. "Rawls rejects it too" says more than "Rawls also discusses this topic".

**Source:** Duke Graduate School Scientific Writing Resource, Lesson 1, "Subjects and Actions": https://sites.duke.edu/scientificwriting/lesson-1-subjects-and-actions/. It states that there is "nothing inherently wrong with nominalizations" and that they are useful for summarising the previous sentence.

## 5. Sentence length from content

Decide what the sentence has to carry, then let it run as long as that takes.

| Content | Natural length | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A result, a turn in the argument, a verdict | 12 words or fewer | One fact, with nothing to subordinate |
| A procedure step, a definition, a plain report | About 15 to 25 | A fact with its circumstances |
| A mechanism, a qualification, a synthesis of several sources | 30 or more | The parts depend on one another and belong in one sentence |

**Revising a flat stretch**

1. Find the sentence that carries the main result. Strip it to subject, verb and figure.
2. Find two neighbouring sentences in which one gives the cause, the condition or the exception for the other. Join them with because, although, whereas or which.
3. Delete the padding that was equalising the lengths ("during the period under study", "respectively", "it should be noted that").
4. Leave the remaining sentences alone.

Do not vary length by quota, break a sentence into fragments, or separate a mechanism from its condition. Pair 6 in `examples.md` shows the difference: the padded version runs 20, 18, 19 and 17 words, and the published version runs 18, 10, 11, 10 and 33 because its facts differ in size.

Disciplines differ in average length. The three articles measured in `evidence.md` (section 5) average between 22 and 41 words per sentence. What they share is the spread.

**Source:** Purdue Online Writing Lab, "Strategies for Variation": https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html. It observes that long sentences "work well for incorporating a lot of information" and that short ones "can often maximize crucial points". The page also recommends alternating short and long sentences for rhythm. This skill adopts the first observation and sets the second aside, since alternation by rule produces a pattern of its own.

## 6. Build order when drafting from notes

1. List the claims, one sentence each, in the order of the argument. This list is the outline.
2. Under each claim, place the evidence from the writer's notes, with its source. A claim with nothing under it goes back to the writer as a question.
3. Add the interpretation. If the notes do not contain it, ask the writer for it.
4. Order each sentence given-new. Add a connective only at a contrast, a concession or a non-obvious result.
5. Hedge the interpretations and give each hedge its reason. Leave the data unhedged.
6. Turn inflated frames and stacked nominalisations into subject-verb statements.
7. Read the sentence lengths and repair any flat stretch as in section 5.
8. Run the avoid list in `SKILL.md`.

## 7. Paragraph check

Five questions for any finished paragraph.

1. Can each sentence be marked C, E or I, and is at least one of each present? (In a results section, C and E suffice.)
2. Does each sentence open with something the previous one supplied?
3. Does every hedge consist of one device and carry its reason?
4. Is the grammatical subject of most sentences the thing studied, with the action in the verb?
5. Do the short sentences carry results or turns, and the long ones mechanisms or qualifications?
